# Chest X-ray findings: reference notes

Educational summaries written for this demo project. They are not clinical guidelines and must not be used for patient care. Replace them with properly licensed guideline text for any serious use.

## Atelectasis
Atelectasis is partial or complete collapse of lung tissue, so that part of the lung holds less air than normal. On a frontal chest X-ray it appears as increased density (whiter area) with signs of volume loss, such as shifted fissures, a raised hemidiaphragm, or crowding of vessels and ribs. Common causes are airway blockage by mucus or a tumour, compression by fluid, and shallow breathing after surgery. Small basal (plate-like) atelectasis is common and often not significant. Lobar collapse in an adult without an obvious cause usually warrants follow-up imaging or CT to look for an obstructing lesion.

## Cardiomegaly
Cardiomegaly means an enlarged cardiac silhouette. On a standard PA film it is usually defined by a cardiothoracic ratio above 0.5 (heart width divided by chest width). AP and supine films magnify the heart, so the ratio overestimates size on those views and the view position should always be stated. Causes include heart failure, valve disease, cardiomyopathy and pericardial effusion. Typical follow-up is clinical correlation and echocardiography rather than repeat X-rays.

## Consolidation
Consolidation is lung tissue in which air in the alveoli has been replaced by fluid, pus, blood or cells. It appears as a homogeneous opacity, often with air bronchograms (dark air-filled bronchi visible inside the white area). Infection (pneumonia) is the most common cause, but haemorrhage, aspiration and some tumours look similar. Interpretation depends on clinical context such as fever, cough and blood tests. Follow-up imaging after treatment is often advised in older patients or smokers to confirm resolution.

## Edema
Pulmonary edema is fluid accumulation in the lung tissue and air spaces. Cardiogenic edema typically shows enlarged upper-lobe vessels, interstitial lines (Kerley B lines), peribronchial cuffing, bilateral perihilar haziness and often pleural effusions and cardiomegaly. Non-cardiogenic causes include acute respiratory distress syndrome and fluid overload. Edema with breathlessness or low oxygen saturation is a time-sensitive finding that needs prompt clinical assessment.

## Effusion
A pleural effusion is fluid in the pleural space. On an upright frontal film the earliest sign is blunting of the costophrenic angle; larger effusions form a meniscus-shaped opacity and can shift the mediastinum away. Supine films hide effusions as a general haziness. Causes include heart failure, infection (parapneumonic effusion), malignancy and kidney or liver disease. Ultrasound confirms and sizes effusions; large or new unexplained effusions often need sampling.

## Emphysema
Emphysema is destruction of alveolar walls, usually from smoking, leading to overinflated lungs. X-ray signs include hyperinflation, flattened hemidiaphragms, an enlarged retrosternal air space on the lateral view, and reduced lung markings or bullae. The chest X-ray is insensitive for mild disease; CT and lung function tests are the standard for assessment.

## Fibrosis
Pulmonary fibrosis is scarring of the lung interstitium. On X-ray it shows reticular (net-like) opacities, usually peripheral and basal, with volume loss; advanced cases show honeycombing. Causes include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, connective tissue disease, drugs and occupational exposures. High-resolution CT is required to characterise the pattern, and comparison with prior films is very useful to judge progression.

## Hernia
In chest X-ray datasets, hernia usually refers to a hiatal hernia: part of the stomach passing through the diaphragm into the chest. It appears as a retrocardiac rounded opacity, often containing an air-fluid level. It is commonly incidental. Very large hernias or symptoms of obstruction need clinical attention.

## Infiltration
"Infiltration" is a non-specific label for an area of increased lung opacity. Many radiologists avoid the term in reports because it does not say what the opacity is, preferring more specific descriptions such as consolidation, ground-glass opacity or interstitial pattern. In the NIH dataset it is the most frequent and noisiest label, so model performance on it is usually the weakest. Reports should describe the opacity and its distribution rather than relying on this label.

## Mass
A lung mass is a focal opacity larger than 3 cm; smaller ones are called nodules. Masses carry a substantial risk of malignancy, especially in older patients and smokers. Features such as irregular or spiculated margins, growth compared with prior imaging, and associated lymph node enlargement raise concern. A new mass on chest X-ray is generally followed up promptly with contrast-enhanced CT.

## Nodule
A pulmonary nodule is a rounded opacity up to 3 cm. Most are benign (old infection, granulomas), but some are early cancers. Chest X-rays miss many nodules and can show false positives such as nipple shadows or rib lesions. Management of incidental nodules depends on size, appearance and patient risk, and normally uses CT-based follow-up recommendations such as the Fleischner Society guidelines. Comparing with prior images to check stability is one of the most useful steps.

## Pleural_Thickening
Pleural thickening is fibrous thickening of the pleura, often after past infection, haemothorax or asbestos exposure. It appears as a smooth or irregular band along the chest wall, sometimes with calcified plaques. Stable, smooth thickening is usually benign. Nodular or progressive thickening, or thickening encasing the lung, needs CT assessment because it can rarely indicate mesothelioma or metastatic disease.

## Pneumonia
Pneumonia is infection of the lung. The X-ray typically shows consolidation or patchy opacities, sometimes with a parapneumonic effusion. The diagnosis is clinical plus radiological: the same appearance can come from other causes, and early or atypical pneumonia can look normal. Severity assessment combines vital signs (oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, blood pressure), age and confusion, for example with the CURB-65 score. Radiological resolution lags behind clinical improvement by several weeks.

## Pneumothorax
A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space, causing the lung to collapse partially or fully. The key sign is a thin visceral pleural line with no lung markings beyond it, usually at the apex on an upright film. Supine films can show a deep sulcus sign instead. A tension pneumothorax shifts the mediastinum away and is a medical emergency, but it is a clinical diagnosis that should not wait for imaging. Chest drains and lines can mimic or hide pneumothorax, and models are known to learn the drain as a shortcut. Any suspected pneumothorax with breathlessness should be escalated immediately.
